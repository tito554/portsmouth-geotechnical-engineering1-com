Crossing from the dense London Clay that underlies parts of Hilsea to the soft alluvial deposits and made ground around the former tidal basins in Portsea tells you everything you need to know about founding in Portsmouth. The city sits on a geological mosaic: Wessex Formation mudstones, chalk of the Portsdown Anticline, and extensive areas of reclaimed land where historic creeks and marshes were filled in during the 19th and 20th centuries. For a mat foundation, this variability demands a design approach that anticipates differential settlement across the slab footprint. In our experience, the critical step is mapping the transition between natural strata and anthropogenic fill — a boundary that runs beneath many of the city's post-war housing estates and commercial developments. We routinely pair the raft design with targeted CPT testing to profile soft zones beneath the proposed formation level, and test pits where we need to visually log the fill composition before committing to bearing assumptions.

A raft in Portsmouth is not a default choice — it's the right choice when variable ground and high groundwater make isolated footings uneconomical.

Process overview

One thing we notice repeatedly on the south coast is that groundwater in Portsmouth doesn't behave like the textbooks suggest. The tidal influence from the Solent, combined with perched water in the made ground layers, creates a fluctuating phreatic surface that can sit less than a metre below finished floor level in parts of Southsea and Old Portsmouth. A raft foundation here has to be designed as a buoyancy-resistant structure, not just a load-spreading plate. We typically model the slab with subgrade reaction moduli that vary zone by zone, reflecting the transition from dense chalk to soft estuarine silts. The reinforcement layout gets concentrated in bands where the stiffness contrast is sharpest — something we learned the hard way on a project near the Continental Ferry Port where early cracking appeared because the ground investigation didn't extend far enough into the chalk. For projects on the more compressible soils east of the M275, we often recommend supplementing the raft with stone columns to homogenise the bearing stratum and accelerate consolidation, and we cross-check settlement predictions with a grain-size analysis because the fines content in Portsmouth's alluvium directly affects long-term creep behaviour.

Local context

Portsmouth sits at an average elevation of just 8 metres above sea level, and large portions of the city — including the entire postcode area PO1 — were built on land reclaimed from the sea between 1850 and 1920. This isn't just historical trivia; it means the founding stratum beneath many structures is a heterogeneous mix of brick rubble, ash, dredged silt, and timber offcuts that can decay and create voids over time. A raft foundation designed without a thorough assessment of this fill risks long-term differential settlement that manifests as step cracking in masonry and binding doors. Add the city's exposure to saline groundwater and occasional frost action, and sulphate attack on buried concrete becomes a durability concern that BS 8500-1 addresses through design chemical class. The structural slab must also account for accidental ground gas migration from buried estuarine deposits — something we've measured in several inner-city sites using in-situ gas monitoring prior to finalising the foundation design.

Quick answers

What is the typical cost range for a raft foundation design on a Portsmouth residential extension?

For a domestic-scale raft foundation design in Portsmouth — typically a rear or side extension on made ground — the structural and geotechnical design package ranges from £810 to £2.890, depending on whether supplementary ground investigation such as trial pits or CPT probing is required and the complexity of the groundwater assessment.

How do you handle the risk of sulphate attack on the raft concrete in Portsmouth?

We take soil and groundwater samples during the ground investigation and test for water-soluble sulphate and magnesium content, plus pH and aggressive CO2. The results are classified per BRE Special Digest 1 and BS 8500-1 to assign a Design Chemical Class (DS-1 through DS-5). In Portsmouth's reclaimed areas we often land at DS-3 or DS-4, which triggers higher cement content, a specified maximum water/cement ratio, and sometimes sulphate-resisting Portland cement.

Can a raft foundation work on the sloping chalk sites near Portsdown Hill?

Yes, but it requires careful stepped or sloped slab detailing to follow the ground profile without excessive cut. The chalk in Portsdown is generally competent, with RQD values often above 70%, but we check for solution features and soft putty chalk pockets that can cause differential stiffness. Where the grade exceeds about 8 degrees, we typically include a slope stability assessment to confirm the excavation is safe before the raft is cast.
